PLAIN-LANGUAGE GLOSSARY · ALL EU COUNTRIES

Public money,
without the jargon.

Short explanations of what our statistics mean, with everyday examples and their limits. These definitions apply across every country; individual national tax rules belong only in their selected-country view.

Household income index (2008 = 100)

In simple terms: Are the average resources available to households per person increasing or shrinking after allowing for price changes? The index makes the value in 2008 equal to 100 for each country.

Example: A reading of 120 means the country’s inflation-adjusted household income per person is about 20% higher than in 2008. A reading of 95 means it is about 5% lower. These are index points, not euros or monthly salaries.

Important: This is a national average per resident, calculated from household and household-serving nonprofit income, after taxes and transfers, adjusted for household consumer prices. It is not the median household, does not show inequality, and a country's 120 cannot be used to claim its people are richer than people in another country showing 110. A year-on-year change compares that country to its own previous year.

Source: Eurostat tepsr_wc310. The methodology uses household gross disposable income per person deflated by the household-consumption price index, with 2008 as the reference year.

“Real” versus “nominal”

Nominal is the amount of money before adjusting for inflation. Real accounts for changes in prices, so it better describes whether the same income can buy more or less than before.

Example: Your salary rises 4%, while consumer prices rise 6%. Your pay increased in euros (nominal), but what it can buy has fallen (real). Exact real growth would be about −1.9%, not necessarily exactly −2%.

Inflation and HICP

Inflation is how fast consumer prices rise on average. HICP is the EU’s harmonised measure, using common statistical rules. The number shown is the average price level across a whole year compared with the previous year's average, not necessarily the December-to-December change. People buying different things may experience different inflation.

Source: Eurostat annual HICP.

National statutory minimum wage

The minimum full-time monthly gross wage established by nationwide law, shown as at 1 January. It is before income tax and employee contributions. Some countries have no nationwide legal minimum but have pay floors set by collective agreements; this is not a zero wage or an absence of wage rules.

COFOG — public spending by purpose

A shared international system that divides government spending into ten purposes: for example, healthcare, transport-related economic activity and social protection. It classifies what money is spent on, not who controls the budget. Roads are mainly in Economic affairs, hospitals in Health, and old-age support in Social protection.

Source: UN COFOG; OECD breakdown.

General government (S13) and consolidated spending

All levels of government together, including central and local authorities and public social-security funds. Consolidated means internal payments between government bodies are handled so the same spending is not accidentally counted twice. Figures are not just a country's central-ministry budget.

Share of public spending

If Health has a 15% share, then 15 out of every 100 units of public expenditure belong to the Health function. This is a distribution, not a statement that an individual taxpayer literally paid 15% of their income tax into hospitals.

Old age, survivors and pension-related functions

Official categories include retirement-related benefits and other old-age or survivors' services. They are already inside Social protection; don't add them again to the national total. National cash-pension statistics can have a different scope.

Gross versus net pay

Gross pay is earnings before employee taxes and contributions. Net pay is what remains after those amounts and applicable credits/benefits. Employer-paid contributions are separate from employee deductions.

Reporting year, tax year and effective date

The reporting year identifies when an expenditure or economic observation occurred. The tax year identifies the laws and thresholds used to estimate a taxpayer’s liability. An official draft budget for a future year is a plan, not proof of actual spending, and must never be silently mixed with different years.

Provisional figures and statistical flags

National statistical offices can revise figures. Provisional means a published observation is subject to revision; a break in series means changes in how the series was measured may affect comparisons. A missing observation is not zero.

Purchasing Power Standard (PPS)

A unit used to adjust for countries' different price levels when comparing actual purchasing-power levels. The household income index on this site is not in PPS, so it cannot establish whether people in one country have greater absolute purchasing power than people in another.

Association versus cause

If government pensions grow while living standards fall, the two may be related—or may be affected by demographics, wages, inflation and other factors. A trend alone does not prove a specific fiscal decision caused the result.
